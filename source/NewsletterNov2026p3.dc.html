Joint-pain spotlight

What is pain keeping you from?

A conversation about treatment starts with the activities you want to keep doing.

Shorter walks. Interrupted sleep. Difficulty getting dressed or opening a jar. Bring those examples to your visit. They are more useful than simply saying, “It still hurts.”

Knee pain: Understanding GAE

Genicular artery embolization (GAE) is a minimally invasive option for selected people with knee pain caused by osteoarthritis. Using imaging and a thin tube called a catheter, a specialist treats abnormal small blood vessels associated with inflammation around the knee. The goal is to reduce pain and improve function without replacing the joint.

An evaluation may be appropriate when treatments such as physical therapy, medication, or injections have not provided enough relief. Your diagnosis, imaging, medical history, and goals help determine whether GAE is a reasonable option. GAE does not regrow cartilage or cure arthritis. Some people still need joint replacement or other treatment, and results vary.

Hip, shoulder, and hand pain: Understanding TAME

Transcatheter arterial microembolization (TAME) also uses imaging and a small catheter to target abnormal vessels associated with certain painful inflammatory conditions. Our team evaluates selected patients with persistent hip, shoulder, and hand symptoms.

Tell us whether hip pain limits walking or sleep, shoulder pain affects reaching or dressing, or hand pain makes gripping and everyday tasks difficult. These symptoms are reasons to seek an evaluation, not proof that TAME is the right treatment. The evidence differs by condition and is still developing. Your consultation should address the likely cause of pain, what you have tried, potential benefits and risks, and alternatives.

A different approach: Peripheral nerve stimulation

Peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) uses a small device to deliver mild electrical impulses near a targeted nerve to help manage certain types of persistent pain. Unlike embolization, it changes pain signaling rather than treating blood vessels. Suitability depends on the source and pattern of your pain.

Your goal belongs in the conversation

“The activity I most want to get back to is ____________.”

Bring that answer to your appointment. Treatment decisions should reflect your life, not just a pain score.

Minimally invasive does not mean risk-free. Ask about bleeding, infection, contrast-related risks where relevant, recovery instructions, and the possibility of limited or no relief.
